For most of my editing, a local sound library works better than a subscription. I've used Artlist, Epidemic Sound, Soundstripe and Soundly in my workflow, and I kept running into the same problem: the sound I needed wasn't there. The sounds I did need were already on my drive, in packs I had collected over the years. What stopped me from using them was organization, and that's a problem a one-time tool can fix.

If you still want a subscription, keep a local library anyway. Your own packs, the free ones other editors share, and the odd sounds no catalog carries will always live outside the platform.

> The short version:
>
> - Subscriptions give you one big catalog and one license to follow, but they only have what they chose to license.
> - Many editors already own sound packs of 2.5, 5 or even 20 GB, plus free packs shared by other editors.
> - The real cost of a local library is organization, and an indexed, searchable library solves it without a monthly fee.

## Subscription vs. local library at a glance

| | Audio subscription | Local sound library |
|---|---|---|
| What you pay | A recurring fee | Whatever you spent on packs, or nothing for free ones, plus a tool if you want one |
| What's in it | What the platform chose to license | Whatever you collect: your packs, free packs, your own recordings |
| License to follow | One company's terms | Each pack's own terms |
| Search | Built into the platform | Needs organizing or an indexing tool |
| After you stop paying | Read the license for files you downloaded | Still yours |

## What subscriptions do well

A subscription is convenient. You search one catalog, download what you need and follow one company's license instead of tracking terms pack by pack. For music in particular, that's a real advantage, and I understand why so many editors start there.

## Where they fell short for me

The catalog only has what the platform chose to license. In my experience across those four platforms, I kept looking for something specific and coming back with "close enough". Trending sounds and meme sounds were the clearest case: the edits that call for them can't wait for a platform to license them, and most of the time they simply weren't available.

The other issue is the payment itself. I didn't want another monthly fee for something I could own. I'd rather pay once for software I keep for good and spend the rest of the budget, or nothing at all, on sounds.

## You probably already have a library

Look at your drives before you subscribe. A lot of editors, me included, have collected sound packs for years: whooshes, impacts, risers, explosions, ambiences, UI clicks. Packs of 2.5, 5 or 20 GB are common, and many of them are very good.

On top of that, many editors and filmmakers share packs for free, with sound effects, music and more. Each pack comes with its own license, so it pays to check what it allows before using it in client work. I wrote a separate guide on [where to find free sound effects and what their licenses allow](https://mysoundlib.com/blog/free-sound-effects-for-video-editing/).

## The real problem is organization

What kept me from using my own packs was finding anything in them. The operating system's file search doesn't help much with folders named `New folder (2)`, packs that nest five levels deep, and file names like `SFX_0142.wav` that say nothing about the sound. Previewing means opening files one by one in a player, and none of it happens inside the editor.

So the library sat there, and I paid for a catalog I could search instead.

## How to keep a local library organized

A few habits make a big library usable:

1. Keep one root folder for all sounds, with one subfolder per pack or source. Don't flatten everything into a single folder; the pack structure carries information.
2. Keep each pack's license file next to its sounds, so you know later what you can use commercially and who needs credit.
3. Don't rename thousands of files by hand. Tag the ones you use often instead.
4. Index the whole thing with a tool that searches by name and tag, previews instantly and works offline.

## If you still want a subscription

Keep the local library next to it. Your own packs, the free packs you collect and the sounds no catalog carries don't belong to any platform, so they shouldn't depend on one. Organize them the same way, with folders per source and the license file in each.

For files you download from a subscription, read what its license says about using them after you cancel before you treat them as part of your permanent library. Terms differ between platforms, and that's the one place where mixing the two can get you in trouble.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use meme or trending sounds in my videos?

It depends on where the sound comes from. Many meme and trending sounds are clips from songs, films or other people's videos, so someone else may own the rights. That matters most in monetized videos and client work, so check the source before you publish.

### Do I need an internet connection for a local library?

No. Once your sounds are on your drive and indexed, searching and previewing them works offline. MySoundLib runs entirely on your computer, with no account.

### Is a local library worth it if I only use a few sounds?

If you use a handful of sounds per project, a folder of favorites may be enough. A searchable library pays off once you have gigabytes of packs and find yourself digging through folders in the middle of an edit.
