Sometimes, but it depends on where the sound comes from, and "everyone uses it" isn't a license. Many meme sounds are clips from songs, films, TV shows or other people's videos, so someone else may own the rights. On YouTube, that can mean a Content ID claim, and only a court can decide whether a particular use is fair use. If a sound is going into a monetized video or client work, use one you have a license for.

This post isn't legal advice. It sums up what YouTube's own help pages say, so you can decide with the facts in front of you.

> The short version:
>
> - Content ID matches uploads against files submitted by copyright owners, and the owner decides whether to block, monetize or track a matching video.
> - Fair use is decided case by case, and YouTube says automated systems like Content ID can't decide it.
> - Giving credit or writing "no infringement intended" doesn't turn a copy into fair use.

## Where meme sounds come from

A meme sound is usually a short clip that spread because people kept reusing it: a line from a film, a few seconds of a song, a reaction from a stream, a sound from a game. The meme status says nothing about who owns it. The original creator, label or studio still does, unless they released it under a license that allows reuse.

That's one reason the big audio subscriptions often don't have the meme sound you're looking for, which I wrote about in [local sound library vs. audio subscriptions](https://mysoundlib.com/blog/local-sound-library-vs-audio-subscriptions/).

## How Content ID handles it

Content ID uses "a database of audio and visual files submitted by copyright owners" and scans uploads automatically. When it finds a match, the video gets a Content ID claim, and the copyright owner's settings decide what happens next. According to YouTube's help page, the owner can block the video, monetize it by running ads "and sometimes sharing revenue with the uploader", or track its viewership statistics. These actions "can be geography-specific", so a video can be blocked in one country and monetized in another ([How Content ID works](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797370)).

In practice, a short meme sound can go unmatched, or it can cost you the revenue of a video you spent days on. You don't control which.

## What YouTube says about fair use

YouTube's fair use page is clear that there's no shortcut. It explains fair use under US law and says that "courts decide fair use cases according to the facts of each unique case" ([Fair use on YouTube](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148)). A few lines from that page are worth knowing before you publish:

- "Giving credit to the owner of a copyrighted work won't by itself turn a non-transformative copy of their material into fair use."
- "Including the phrase 'no infringement intended' won't automatically protect you from a claim of copyright infringement."
- Labeling a video "for entertainment purposes only" is unlikely to change the outcome, and non-profit use isn't an automatic defense either.
- "Automated systems like Content ID can't decide fair use", because it's a case-by-case decision that only courts can make.

So the description-box disclaimer you see under so many videos doesn't do what people hope it does.

## Before you use a meme sound

| Where the sound comes from | Risk on a monetized video | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| A clip from a song, film, TV show or game | High: the owner may have it in Content ID | Use a licensed alternative or recreate it |
| Another creator's video or stream | Depends on the creator | Ask for permission or skip it |
| A sound pack shared by a creator | Depends on the pack's license | Read the license file before using it |
| YouTube Audio Library | Low on YouTube: Google says these sounds won't be claimed | Download it from YouTube Studio |
| Your own recording or sound design | None from others | Use it freely |

## Safer ways to get the same effect

You can still make an edit feel like a meme without borrowing the exact clip:

- Use sounds you have a license for. The YouTube Audio Library is built for monetized YouTube videos, and I compared it with other free sources in [free sound effects for video editing](https://mysoundlib.com/blog/free-sound-effects-for-video-editing/).
- Read the terms of meme-style sound packs that creators share. Each comes with its own license, so check it before using the sounds in paid work.
- Recreate the moment with a similar reaction, timing or effect that you record or design yourself, so nobody else holds a claim to it.

Keep the sounds you're allowed to use in their own folder, with the license file next to them, and tag them so they're easy to find. In MySoundLib, a tag like `cleared` or `youtube-ok` lets you filter for safe sounds when you search over your editor.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will a two-second meme sound get my video claimed?

It might or might not. Content ID matches against files the rights owners submitted, so the outcome depends on whether that sound's owner registered it and how they set up their claims.

### Is a Content ID claim the end of the video?

It depends on what the owner chose: the video can be blocked, monetized by the owner or only tracked, and the choice can differ by country. YouTube's help pages explain the options in detail.

### Is it fair use if my video is a parody or a review?

It can be, but YouTube's page says fair use is decided case by case by courts. If it matters for your channel or a client, ask someone qualified before you publish.
